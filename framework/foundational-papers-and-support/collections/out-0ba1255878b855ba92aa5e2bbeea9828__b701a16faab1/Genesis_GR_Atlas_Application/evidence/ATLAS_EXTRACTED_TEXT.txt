Chapter 1
Executive summary
The Atlas of Becoming began with a deceptively simple question: What does it mean for some-
thing to remain itself while becoming something else? Over thirty research notes, the question
evolved into a precise investigation of mathematical identity, transformation, persistence, in-
formation loss, proof, and historical record. This report presents the resulting foundations as
a single readable account. It is intended for a mathematically literate reader, but it explains
the central ideas before introducing formal notation.
The project’s most important finding is a separation of things that are often treated as
though they were identical. Two objects may have the same structure without sharing a
history. Two histories may produce the same result without having the same intermediate
steps. Two proofs may establish the same mathematical claim without being the same proof.
And a mathematically correct proof does not, by itself, certify that a claimed historical event
occurred.
These are not philosophical distinctions alone. They can be expressed by specific mathematical
constructions and tested in concrete cases. The Atlas uses categories and functors to describe
preservation of structure; directed paths and causal partial orders to describe recorded trans-
formations; linear algebra and finite counting to study what can be recovered; rewriting and
trace theory to compare histories; and versioned certificates to distinguish genuine verification
from assertions of verification.
The research produced the following principal results:
1. Structural identity is relative to a chosen structure. A triangle can change its lengths
without changing its abstract connectivity. The answer to “is it the same?” depends on
whether we mean geometry, graph structure, topology, or some other specified invariant.
2. Persistence is stronger than restoration. A feature can be present at the beginning
and end of a history while disappearing between them. Equal endpoint measurements
do not prove unbroken transport.
3. The mathematical result does not uniquely identify the path. Distinct ordered histo-
ries can have the same evaluated transformation or the same final state. Preserving the
path requires an additional historical record.
4. Recoverability admits exact criteria. In a finite-dimensional linear setting, a transfor-
mation output together with an auxiliary record reconstructs the input precisely when
their kernels have trivial intersection. For arbitrary histories, exact recovery is charac-
terized by whether different target histories can produce identical retained evidence.

5. Causal order differs from recorded order. Several valid serializations may express
the same partial order; the absence of a causal relation, however, does not establish that
the corresponding transformations commute.
6. Safe interchange is conditional. Two operations may be exchangeable at one state and
not at another. For partial operations, admissibility and the equality of the reached states
must both be checked.
7. A minimal sufficient observation can be constructed for finite deterministic sys-
tems. Iteratively separating states that differ in enabledness or future observable behav-
ior yields the coarsest stable refinement of an initial observation.
8. Proof checking, version migration, and historical authentication have different
trust requirements. A small checker can validate a formal derivation relative to stated
rules and assumptions. It cannot establish authorship, timing, or occurrence without
independent evidence.
These conclusions are supported by proofs of conditional propositions, counterexamples, and
finite computational experiments. For example, a four-event experiment examined all 24 pos-
sible execution orders and the 16 shortest derivations that reverse their order. A separate
observational-refinement example showed why one pass of an algorithm was insufficient: the
actual stable refinement required a second pass. Such tests confirm the examples, not the gen-
eral theory; the general claims stand or fall by their mathematical arguments.
What was completed: the foundational research program through Research Note 030; a con-
solidated specification; multiple proof sketches and complete elementary proofs; finite ver-
ification experiments; a proof-record prototype; and an archival notebook that records the
project’s evolution. What was not completed: an integrated, release-ready Atlas Engine; a
fully formalized general higher-category coherence theory; a complete authentication system;
or independent peer review. The principal mathematical ingredients are largely established
work. The project’s distinctive aim is their careful integration into a reproducible, provenance-
preserving architecture. The originality of that integration remains to be evaluated against the
broader literature.
The central conclusion can be stated without specialized vocabulary:
Knowing what something became is not the same as knowing how it became
that way. The history, the result, the justification, and the evidence for the
history must be kept distinguishable.
This report is a foundational research submission, not a declaration of a proven new uni-
versal theory or a completed software product. It supplies the definitions and arguments nec-
essary for another researcher to understand, challenge, reproduce, and extend the work.

Chapter 2
The question: when is a changing thing
still the same thing?
2.1 A triangle, three answers
Imagine drawing a triangle on paper, then stretching the paper. The angles and lengths change,
but it still has three corners and three sides connected in a cycle. Is it the same triangle? A
geometer concerned with congruence might answer no. Someone considering the incidence
graph—the abstract record of which vertices connect to which other vertices—might answer
yes. Someone concerned only with topology might go further: the outline of any nondegener-
ate triangle is topologically a circle.
The triangle has not delivered a contradictory answer. We asked three different questions
under the same everyday wording. The first commitment of the Atlas is therefore to specify
the structure that matters before asking whether identity survives. In mathematics that
often means choosing an invariant: a measurable or describable property preserved by the
transformations under consideration.
This insight is elementary, but it is essential. Without it, one can declare that two states are
“the same” while silently changing the criterion of sameness from one line of reasoning to the
next. The Atlas deliberately refuses that ambiguity.
The initial notebook introduced a provisional “Becoming Object,” written

records 𝑃. This tuple served as an organizing idea rather than as a finalized axiomatic object.
It made a key distinction visible at the outset: a transformation permitted by a mathemat-
ical model is not necessarily a transformation that actually occurred. Admissibility and
historical evidence are different matters (RN 001).

The distinction matters beyond geometry. Two programs can return the same answer for an in-

while relying on different lemmas. The Atlas aims to preserve those differences when they are
important to explanation or reconstruction.
2.3 Why the path matters
A crucial contribution during the early development came from Genesis, who proposed com-
paring two chains of transformations with equivalent beginning and ending structures
but different intermediate states. This was recorded as the Path Hypothesis (RN 004): an
account of restoration cannot necessarily be reduced to a test of endpoint equivalence. One
must ask which paths were admissible, which transformations they composed, and what in-
formation passed through them.
Consider a journey from one room to another. The rooms tell us the starting and ending loca-
tions, but they do not tell us the route. Different routes may have different costs, risks, interme-
diate experiences, and physical consequences. Likewise, two mathematical histories can end
in equivalent states without being the same history.
In geometry, holonomy offers a vivid example. Transporting a vector around a closed loop
on a curved surface can return it to the starting point with a changed orientation. The loca-
tion is restored while the transported information has changed. The Atlas does not claim to
have invented holonomy; it uses the example to show why the journey can be mathematically
significant.

2.4 The guiding commitments
The project established four enduring commitments. First, objects, representations, and histo-
ries must be distinguishable. Second, claims about preservation must refer to the actual trans-
formation, not merely to the shapes of its endpoints. Third, a mathematical proof must be kept
separate from the claim that a historical event occurred. Fourth, comparing or compressing
histories must not silently erase the original records.
These commitments form the conceptual thread through all thirty research notes. They are
modest individually; together they define the kind of mathematical account the project seeks
to build.

Chapter 3
Persistence, restoration, and the mathe-
matical meaning of a witness
3.1 Persistence is a statement about the entire route
Consider a loop of string. Cut it open, obtaining an interval. Join its ends again, obtaining a
loop. The loop exists at the beginning and the end. But the closed-loop feature did not exist
continuously throughout the process. That difference is captured by a topological measure-
ment called the first Betti number, which counts independent one-dimensional cycles in the
examples under discussion.
𝛽1(circle) = 1,
𝛽1(interval) = 0,
𝛽1(restored circle) = 1.
This is a schematic comparison of spaces, not an assertion that the act of cutting is a particular
continuous map. The point is the change in the measured feature: the endpoints agree, but
uninterrupted topological persistence has failed (RN 003).
That motivates three distinct statements:
• Endpoint equivalence: the first and last states exhibit equivalent chosen structures.
• Stepwise persistence: every actual intermediate transformation transports that struc-
ture by an invertible map.
• Restoration: the endpoint structure returns even though stepwise preservation failed
along the selected route.
A careful mathematician asks which of these claims is being made. An identical measurement
at two times may be evidence of restoration, but not proof of continuity between the measure-
ments.
3.2 The role of a functor
A category is a collection of objects together with arrows between them, subject to rules for
identity arrows and composition. An arrow might represent a transformation, a map, a pro-
gram, or another type-correct relationship. A functor sends those objects and arrows into
another category while preserving identities and composition.

3.3 The preservation category
The first decisive mathematical construction was the preservation category 𝒲𝐹. It keeps ev-
ery object of 𝒞, but includes only those arrows 𝑓for which 𝐹(𝑓) is invertible. This is a wide sub-

and composition because functors preserve identities and the composition of isomorphisms
remains an isomorphism.
This construction should not be confused with a groupoid. An arrow 𝑓need not be invertible
in the original category just because 𝐹(𝑓) is invertible after information has been discarded.
For example, a functor may forget a distinction that prevented inversion at the original level.
The Atlas must state which structure is preserved and at what level (RN 006, RN 030).

The category-theoretic definitions and propositions give a rigorous language for structure rel-

Chapter 4
A history is not its result
4.1 The simplest loss of historical information
Suppose a computer begins with a number 𝑥and performs two arithmetic operations: add one,
then subtract one. It returns 𝑥. Another computer does nothing and also returns 𝑥. The final
results agree, but the executions differ. If our only record is the final number, the two histories
cannot be distinguished.
Even recording the complete composite function does not necessarily help. The composition
“subtract one after adding one” is the identity function, as is the instruction “do nothing.” A
record of the composite function forgets the decomposition into operations. To recover the
decomposition, the operations themselves must be recorded. This is one of the central insights
of the project: evaluation is generally a many-to-one operation on histories.
The Atlas formalizes a recorded mathematical process with a directed multigraph. Vertices
represent recorded states, and each directed edge identifies a specific admissible transition.
A multigraph permits two distinct edges between the same pair of states, because two events
may have the same endpoints without being the same event. A history is a finite sequence of
composable edges. The empty sequence is also permitted; it records that no transition was
taken (RN 007).
A free path category takes those vertices as objects and the finite paths as arrows. Its critical
property is that paths are not automatically identified merely because their composite effect is
the same. Evaluation then sends a path to the actual composite transformation in the chosen
mathematical model:
𝐸∶Path(𝐺) ⟶𝒞.
The map 𝐸preserves composition, so it is a functor. It may fail to be faithful—meaning dif-
ferent paths may be sent to exactly the same transformation. The historical layer retains the
distinction that evaluation forgets.

4.3 Proofs have histories too
Mathematics presents another layer of the same problem. Imagine two students proving the
same statement. One proof cites a theorem directly; another derives the statement from three
intermediate lemmas. The conclusions match. The proof trees do not. Likewise, a long proof
written one way may be equivalent to a shorter proof after inserting or removing trivial iden-
tity steps.
The notebook introduced proof-bearing certificates whose conclusions, derivation trees,
and historical recording events are separately retained (RN 008–009). A proof-composition
operation can combine certificates for successive transformations. But the identity of a proof
tree is not identical to the identity of the transformation it certifies; nor is the identity of a
stored proof text the same as the historical claim about when or by whom it was produced.
To allow limited simplification of proof syntax, the research defined a structural equivalence
relation identifying reassociation and identity-proof insertion while retaining independent
derivations unless a justified comparison exists. With suitable typing and congruence rules,
the resulting proof-bearing arrows form a category, and forgetting the proof produces a func-
tor into the earlier preservation category.
This is an example of how a mathematical quotient should be used responsibly. We may decide
that two proof expressions are equivalent for a given purpose while still retaining their original
syntax trees in the archive. The equivalence class supports mathematical comparison; the
original records support provenance.

What can be recovered from incomplete

5.1 The difference between losing information and losing access to it
Suppose a measurement device records the horizontal coordinate of a point but discards the
vertical coordinate. From the measurement alone, many original points are possible. If a sec-
ond device retained the vertical coordinate, the pair of records would recover the point exactly.
The first operation did not preserve all information in its output; the total system may never-
theless make the original state recoverable.
The Atlas investigated this distinction using linear algebra because the result is exact, transpar-
ent, and testable (RN 010).
Let 𝑉be a finite-dimensional vector space of original states, 𝐴∶𝑉→𝑊a linear measurement
or transformation, and 𝑆∶𝑉→𝑅an auxiliary record. What we retain is the pair
𝐽(𝑣) = (𝐴(𝑣), 𝑆(𝑣)).
The kernel of a linear map is the collection of inputs it sends to zero. If two distinct inputs
differ by a vector lying in the kernel, they produce identical outputs. Exact recovery requires
that no nonzero difference be invisible to both records.
The resulting criterion is
ker(𝐴) ∩ker(𝑆) = {0}.
Why is this sufficient? If two inputs 𝑣and 𝑤give the same pair of records, their difference be-
longs to both kernels. If the intersection contains only zero, then 𝑣= 𝑤; the records distinguish
every input. Why is it necessary? If a nonzero vector lies in both kernels, it and zero produce
the same records. No decoder receiving only those records can know which input occurred.
This is an application of standard linear algebra, not an independently new theorem. But it
gives the project a genuinely useful test: it distinguishes information that is absent from a
single output from information that remains reconstructible through retained side data.

5.2 How much auxiliary information is needed?
Suppose 𝑉has dimension 𝑛, and the primary map 𝐴has rank 𝑟. The rank–nullity theorem says
that 𝐴loses access to 𝑛−𝑟independent dimensions of the input. An auxiliary linear record

The conclusion is subtle: large records are not necessarily informative records. They
must distinguish the ambiguities left by the original observation.
5.3 Recovering a feature without recovering the whole state
Often the investigator does not need every detail of the source. Perhaps only a selected quantity
𝐿(𝑣) matters. The Atlas gives a weaker criterion for recovering that feature from the total record
𝐽:
ker(𝐽) ⊆ker(𝐿).
This says that any difference invisible to the retained observations must also be invisible to
the desired feature. When that condition holds, a function can recover 𝐿(𝑣) from 𝐽(𝑣) even if it
cannot reconstruct 𝑣itself.
This distinction generalizes the project beyond a crude all-or-nothing notion of information
loss. A transformation may destroy some distinctions, preserve others, and support recon-
struction of still others when combined with an auxiliary record. Every recoverability claim
must therefore say what is to be recovered and which evidence is available.

The project also examined rewriting systems, in which a state is repeatedly transformed until

each starting state. Termination means the process cannot continue forever. Confluence means
that divergent valid reductions can eventually be reconciled.
Imagine a starting expression 𝑋reducing by two routes, 𝑋→𝐴→𝑁and 𝑋→𝐵→𝑁. Both

For any fixed initial state whose complete reductions all reach the same normal form, the “send

one complete raw history was possible. It can recover a class of histories only if the relevant
class is unique. Additional provenance can change the answer; uniqueness of the normal form
itself cannot (RN 020).
The report therefore distinguishes three questions that are too easily confused: Can we recover
the original state? Can we recover a desired feature? Can we recover the sequence of events?
The mathematical tests are different because the objects to be recovered are different.

Causality, chronology, and multiple valid

6.1 A list of events is not a causal explanation
Suppose a researcher defines a mathematical object, proves a proposition using that definition,
and independently records a measurement. The proof depends on the definition. The measure-
ment need not depend on either. A chronological list nevertheless places all three events in a
total order, even though only one dependency is established.
The Atlas models a causal history by a partial order (𝐸, ⪯) on event occurrences. The relation
𝑒⪯𝑓means that 𝑒is a prerequisite of 𝑓, or that they are the same event. It is reflexive, anti-
symmetric, and transitive. Two distinct events may be incomparable: neither is recorded as
preceding the other in this causal sense.
This does not prove that the events were physically or statistically independent. A missing
causal edge may reflect incomplete records. The model expresses recorded constraints, not
omniscient knowledge of the world (RN 013).

6.2 How two serializations can agree
An established theorem about finite partially ordered sets says that any two linear extensions
can be connected by swapping adjacent incomparable events. This tells us something precise:
if two orderings differ only in the placement of events for which the poset imposes no order,
they describe the same underlying partial-order constraints.

6.3 When order truly does not change the result
There is also a useful positive theorem. Suppose each event acts as a total transformation on
a common state space, and the transformations associated with every pair of incomparable
events commute. Then every linear extension of the finite causal poset induces the same com-
posite transformation.
The proof uses the adjacent-swap theorem: move from one linear extension to another by
swaps of incomparable neighbors. Each swap preserves the composite because the correspond-
ing operations commute. After finitely many swaps, the entire evaluation agrees.
This result is valuable because it identifies a sufficient condition for path-independent eval-
uation. The recorded histories still differ as historical sequences; they simply become equiva-
lent under the chosen mathematical evaluation.
The condition is deliberately limited. For typed arrows, the two routes must compose in the
appropriate source and target categories. For partial maps, both orders must be defined on
the relevant states. For general rewriting systems, the ability to reach a common result after
further rewriting is not the same as the immediate equality of two composites.

When historical records from two sources are combined, their precedence constraints must
be checked. If one record says 𝑎strictly precedes 𝑏and another says 𝑏strictly precedes 𝑎, the
combined strict relation contains a cycle. It cannot describe a partial order on distinct events.
A valid merge therefore requires compatible identities for shared event occurrences and an
acyclic combined strict precedence graph. After combining its edges, the transitive closure
yields the merged partial order. Reflexive self-relations should not be mistaken for strict causal
cycles.

Chapter 7
When proofs of equivalence disagree
7.1 Another level above paths
The earlier chapters compared transformation histories. The research then encountered a
further issue: even when two histories are mathematically equivalent, there may be several
ways of proving that equivalence. Do those proofs agree? Or is their difference significant?
Imagine rearranging three independently executable operations 𝑎, 𝑏, 𝑐into reverse order.
There are two shortest ways to do so using adjacent swaps:
𝑎𝑏𝑐⟶𝑏𝑎𝑐⟶𝑏𝑐𝑎⟶𝑐𝑏𝑎
and
𝑎𝑏𝑐⟶𝑎𝑐𝑏⟶𝑐𝑎𝑏⟶𝑐𝑏𝑎.
Each route uses three swaps. Each takes the same starting sequence to the same final sequence.
If we already know that the operations commute appropriately, both establish equality of the
evaluated transformations. Yet the derivations of that equality are not literally the same se-
quence of steps (RN 015–016).
This motivates a language in which transformations of states are accompanied by transfor-
mations between transformation paths. In higher category theory, the first are called 1-
morphisms and the second 2-morphisms. A square whose two routes are certified to agree can
be represented by a 2-cell 𝛼∶𝑝⇒𝑞.
But the existence of 2-cells connecting histories does not automatically show that different
composites of those 2-cells agree. That is the coherence problem. The research was careful
not to declare a complete higher-category theory merely by drawing commuting squares: the
precise typing, composition, and coherence equations are part of the mathematics, not optional
decoration.
7.2 The coherence boundary
For three adjacent swaps, the two minimal reversal proofs are related to the familiar braid or
Yang–Baxter pattern. In an appropriate algebraic setting, one can impose a relation equating
the two ways of passing three operations past one another. But the equation does not follow
from the mere existence of pairwise swap certificates.

8.1 Two operations that sometimes commute
Real processes rarely operate without preconditions. A bank withdrawal requires sufficient
funds; a chemical reaction requires suitable conditions; a software command may require a
file to exist. In mathematical terms, these operations are often better represented as partial
functions than as functions defined on every conceivable input.
A partial transformation 𝑎∶𝑆⇀𝑆has a domain dom(𝑎) of states on which it can execute. When
two partial operations are composed, the composite is defined only where every required in-
termediate step is defined.

𝑎(𝑥) = 𝑥+ 1
(𝑥≥0),
𝑏(𝑥) = 𝑥−1

For partial maps, equality requires agreement of outputs and agreement of domains. Restrict-

rejects vague assurances that two operations “commute wherever they both work” as a global
interchange certificate.

8.3 Why a local certificate needs its context
A valid swap certificate should record the actual incoming state, the identities of the two event
occurrences, the output state reached by each order, the evidence for definedness and equality,
and the version of the transformation definitions under which it was checked.
Without the incoming state, we may apply a valid certificate at the wrong point in a history.
Without event identifiers, two identical operation names can be confused. Without version
information, a later change to the operation’s permitted domain can make an old certificate
inapplicable. Without checkable evidence, the certificate is merely an assertion.
A certified-swap graph can be built for a fixed starting state. Its vertices are executable histories;
edges join pairs related by a valid contextual exchange. Every connected component consists
of histories that lead to the same final state. The converse fails: two histories can end at the
same state without being connected by swaps, particularly when their lengths or event counts
differ.
The project therefore distinguishes endpoint equivalence, semantic equality of compos-
ites, and connectivity under explicitly certified local swaps. All three are useful; none
should be silently substituted for another.

Chapter 9
Observational equivalence and the safe
loss of detail
9.1 What if the full state cannot be seen?
Sometimes an investigator cannot observe the full mathematical state. A sensor may report
only whether a quantity is positive. An interface may reveal an output label while hiding in-
ternal variables. Two systems can look identical now yet behave differently under the next
permitted operation.
Let 𝑞∶𝑆→𝑄be an observation map. We say that 𝑥and 𝑦are observationally equivalent when
𝑞(𝑥) = 𝑞(𝑦). The critical question is whether this equivalence survives every future admissible
operation (RN 024).
Simply observing the same current value is not enough. Suppose two hidden states both report
“ready,” but a command is enabled in only one of them. Their behavior differs in a way the
initial observation concealed. Or suppose the command works at both states but produces
visibly different outputs. In either case, the observation is not adequate for predicting the
future.
9.2 Strong transition congruence
The Atlas uses a strong transition congruence to capture this requirement. It is an equivalence
relation on states with two properties: equivalent states agree about whether every operation
is enabled, and, whenever an operation is enabled, their resulting states remain equivalent.
The word congruence means that the equivalence is respected by the operations. If the condi-
tion holds for a single operation, it continues to hold for a sequence of operations by induction.
Thus equivalent states remain observationally indistinguishable under every finite common
continuation.
This yields a mathematically defensible account of what information may be forgotten. We
can merge states into equivalence classes only if the discarded distinctions cannot affect
the chosen future-observable behavior. We still preserve the original histories separately
when historical reconstruction is required.
For partial operations, enabledness is part of the observable behavior even when no output
value exists. An operation being defined at one state and undefined at another is a meaningful
difference.

9.3 Constructing the smallest sufficient observation
The next question was algorithmic: given an initial, possibly too-coarse observation, how do
we find the coarsest refinement that is safe under all future operations?
For a finite deterministic system, begin by grouping states with the same observation. Then
repeatedly split any group when its states differ in which operations are enabled or in the
groups to which those operations send them. Continue until no further split occurs.
At termination, states in the same group are guaranteed to have matching future enabledness
and observation for every finite operation word. In other words, the resulting partition is
stable. It is coarsest among the stable partitions that respect the original observations: it does
not distinguish states unnecessarily for this stated purpose.
In symbols, let 𝑅0 be equality under 𝑞. Define 𝑅𝑛+1 by retaining from 𝑅𝑛only those pairs whose
enabledness agrees and whose enabled successors are 𝑅𝑛-equivalent for every operation. The
sequence becomes finer:
𝑅0 ⊇𝑅1 ⊇𝑅2 ⊇⋯.
On a finite state space, each strict refinement increases the number of distinct blocks. This
cannot happen more than a finite number of times, so the process reaches a fixed point 𝑅∗(RN
025).
The proof of minimality is also elementary. Any safe equivalence that refines the original
observation remains inside every successive refinement: if two states can safely be merged,
their enabledness and safe successor equivalence cannot force them apart. Consequently, no
coarser safe partition than the eventual fixed point has been overlooked.

9.5 Why “minimal” is always relative
The stable quotient is minimal only relative to the chosen observation map, the available op-
eration alphabet, and the exact meaning and version of each operation. Add a new operation
that reveals hidden information, and the quotient may have to become finer. Change which
outputs matter, and the definition of sufficient information changes.
The algorithm does not compress the event history. A quotient of states is a statement about
future-behavior equivalence; it is not a record of which historical path reached a state. That
distinction prevents a practical system from sacrificing provenance in the name of state-space
optimization.
The finite deterministic construction is well established in automata and transition-system the-
ory. Extending it to nondeterministic or probabilistic processes requires specifying a new no-
tion of behavioral equivalence, such as a particular form of bisimulation or trace equivalence.
The notebook records these extensions as open rather than quietly assuming they have been
proved.

10.3 Trace theory: remembering causal equivalence
A trace monoid is an algebraic model in which words are considered equivalent when speci-
fied adjacent independent symbols may be exchanged. For example, if operations 𝑎and 𝑏are
declared independent, the words 𝑎𝑏and 𝑏𝑎belong to the same trace. A trace is an equivalence
class of words, not necessarily the one chronological sequence that happened.
The mathematical advantage is that many apparent event orders can be represented as one
causal or dependency structure. The archival danger is equally clear: replacing a raw event
sequence with its trace class loses the specific order of occurrences when more than one word
belongs to the class.
A canonical representative—such as the lexicographically first word in a trace class—makes
comparison convenient. It is not the true history unless independent evidence happens to
establish that the canonical order was the one executed.

10.5 Three separate compression goals
An Atlas user might want to reduce file size while preserving one of three different things.
Goal A: preserve the mathematical output. Then it may suffice to store a composite func-
tion or final state, if no later question requires the sequence. This is often the cheapest option,
but it gives up historical reconstruction.
Goal B: preserve a trace or causal class. Then a canonical representative of that class may
suffice, provided the independence relation, event dictionary, and model version are retained.
Different actual orders are intentionally treated as equivalent.
Goal C: preserve the actual sequence. Then the record must identify the raw occurrence
order, either directly or through a provably lossless encoding relative to available side infor-
mation. This is the goal when historical fidelity matters.
A reliable mathematical notebook may use all three representations for different purposes.
The critical design rule is to retain the most informative original record and treat cheaper
abstractions as derived views, unless an explicit archival policy permits a genuine loss of detail.
10.6 Why trace equivalence cannot be assumed everywhere
Fixed trace theory assumes a predeclared, context-independent independence relation among
event types. RN 019 and RN 023 showed why that assumption fails for many partial operations:
whether two operations can be exchanged may depend on the incoming state. A bank-like
increment/decrement pair that is exchangeable in the interior of the allowed range can fail at
its boundary.
The Atlas therefore distinguishes global trace classes from state-indexed certified-swap
classes. It can use trace monoids as a simple baseline while moving to certificates for partial
systems. A missing dependency in a recorded poset is not proof of independence, and agree-
ment of endpoint values is not proof of a valid adjacent swap.

11.1 Mathematical documents change after proofs are written
A theory is rarely frozen at the moment its first propositions are proved. Definitions become
more precise, assumptions are revised, and notation migrates to a new formal language. These
changes raise a practical question: can a result accepted under an earlier version still be
trusted?
There are at least three possibilities. The changed definition is irrelevant to the proof, so the
old certificate remains valid. The proof depends on a changed definition, so the certificate
must be rechecked. Or rechecking shows that the old derivation is no longer valid under the
new rules. The third outcome does not imply that the derivation was invalid under its original
assumptions (RN 026).
The Atlas proposes a versioned dependency record for each formal certificate. It identifies the
definitions, axioms, inference rules, interpretations, and checker behavior on which the certifi-
cate actually relies. When a new theory version changes a set of dependencies Δ, a certificate
with a complete dependency set 𝐷need not be invalidated if
𝐷∩Δ = ∅,
provided its unchanged dependencies retain the same meaning. This is safe incremental
reuse, not a magical guarantee that one never has to re-examine proofs. Its soundness depends
on the completeness of 𝐷and the trustworthiness of the checker.
The finite version-migration test had four certificates. Two were affected by a simulated re-
vision and two were unaffected. The test supported the intended behavior: unrelated work
need not be discarded, while dependent work must not be silently carried forward without
validation.
11.2 A proof translated to a new theory is a new artifact
Suppose version 𝑣contains a proof and version 𝑤uses a different proof language. A translation
may carry an old proof into the new language. If it is well defined on all proof expressions
and preserves identity and composition, the translation can be studied as a functor between
categories of proofs (RN 027).

11.4 A small independent checker
The notebook implemented a deliberately small formal proof checker. Its role was not to solve
mathematics automatically, but to demonstrate a trust boundary. A translator, generator, or
model may propose a proof. A separate checker then verifies its syntax and inference steps
against an explicit calculus.
One minimal example used propositional formulas, assumptions or axioms, and modus po-
nens: from 𝐴and 𝐴⇒𝐵, derive 𝐵. Soundness follows by induction over the derivation: ac-
cepted axiom lines are true under the stipulated interpretation, and each valid modus ponens
step preserves truth.
The prototype accepted a valid derivation and rejected several intentionally invalid cases,
including forged rules and missing assumptions.
A later translator test checked forty-six
valid translations and three deliberately invalid target proofs. These are finite implementa-
tion checks; they do not transform the small prototype into a mature general-purpose proof
assistant (RN 028–029).
11.5 What the checker can and cannot certify
A mathematical checker can verify that a formal argument follows from declared assumptions
and rules, subject to the checker’s soundness and correct interpretation. It cannot generally
infer whether an axiom is scientifically appropriate. It cannot authenticate an author or prove
that a claimed research event occurred. It cannot prove its own security merely by accepting
proof objects.
Consider two records containing the same valid proof text. One accurately names its author;
the other falsely attributes the proof. A checker that examines only mathematical inference
will accept both. The mathematics is the same; the historical statements differ. Authenticity
requires separate evidence, potentially including signed records, trusted timestamps, external
corroboration, and an explicit threat model.
The Atlas’s core lesson is therefore to divide responsibility: the kernel checks mathematics;
the provenance archive records claims and evidence of history; the migration layer
proposes transformations of certificates but cannot declare itself trustworthy.

Chapter 12
The proposed Atlas Engine
12.1 Turning the research into a system
The original project chose three deliverables: a mathematical monograph, a reference imple-
mentation called the Atlas Engine, and a reproducible experimental archive. The thirty foun-
dational notes establish the first of these only in research-notebook form. They also contain
small prototypes and executable experiments, but not yet an integrated, release-ready engine.
The final foundational note (RN 030) specifies a deliberately restricted first version. This is a
wise engineering boundary: a small system whose guarantees can be checked is more useful
than an ambitious system that silently claims to authenticate events or solve arbitrary higher-
category equivalence.
An Atlas Engine record should be able to name a mathematical object or event, identify its
theory and interpretation versions, refer to immutable source material, list its dependencies,
carry checkable proof evidence, and record verification outcomes as separate events. The
record should distinguish what the system itself checked from what an external source merely
claimed.
12.2 A practical data architecture
A simple implementation can organize information into five related stores or layers.
Object and theory registry. Records the mathematical state types, operations, source and
target types, observation maps, axioms, and versions in which they have a defined meaning.
A proof relying on an operation cannot be interpreted without this registry.
Raw event history. Stores distinct event occurrences and their chronological or causal links.
Each event points to its transformation meaning, source state or type, and evidence. It must
support repeated event types without collapsing their occurrences.
Mathematical evaluation. Computes composite transformations, observed outputs, stable
quotients, and certified equivalence classes. These are reproducible derived views rather than
substitutes for the raw record.
Proof and verification registry. Stores formal derivations, the checker and rules used, and
versioned acceptance or rejection outcomes. A new check creates a new verification event; it
does not erase the old one.

15.1 The answer to the founding question
The founding question asked what it means for something to remain itself while becoming
something else. The Atlas does not return one universal metaphysical answer. Instead, it pro-
vides an organized collection of mathematical questions whose answers depend on what is
meant by “itself”:
If we mean structure, specify a functor or invariant and determine what the transformation
preserves. If we mean unbroken persistence, examine the induced maps throughout the
actual path. If we mean historical continuity, retain the sequence of recorded event occur-
rences. If we mean recoverability, specify the evidence and the distinctions the decoder must
reproduce. If we mean justification, retain the mathematical proof and its assumptions. If
we mean authenticity, require evidence appropriate to the historical claim.
These answers differ because the questions differ. The Atlas makes those differences explicit
so that legitimate mathematical agreement does not create false historical agreement.
The research’s most concise conclusion is therefore this:
Two things can become structurally equivalent without having followed
equivalent histories. Two histories can be mathematically equivalent with-
out being the same recorded history. And a valid proof about a history is not
proof that the history occurred.
That conclusion is supported by the project’s definitions, counterexamples, conditional theo-
rems, and finite experiments. It is not an attempt to derive all aspects of identity from a single
new law.

ples that made abstract definitions concrete. Second, it checked numerical and combinatorial
counts that might be easy to get wrong by hand. Third, it supplied deliberately invalid inputs to
expose false conclusions. These roles must be distinguished from formal mathematical proof.
A finite enumeration can establish a finite case exhaustively, but it cannot prove a theorem
over arbitrary state spaces or all possible categories without additional reasoning.

programs for Notes 017–019, 023, 025, 026, 028 and 029 were executed again. Their execution
completed successfully in the current preparation environment. The tests are still small proto-
types rather than a single integrated Atlas Engine regression suite.

C.2 Errors that were worth retaining
Two mistakes have particular pedagogical value.
The trace-class count. An early expectation predicted six equivalence classes in the four-
event trace experiment. Full enumeration found fourteen. The corrected distribution—eight
classes of size one, four of size two, two of size four—is preserved along with the revision. The
episode illustrates why an intuitive picture of “independent events” can undercount the classes
when independence is limited to particular pairs.
The observational partition.
The first refinement of the six-state system produced four
blocks. That looked plausible as a stable result, but a second operation revealed differences
within two of the blocks. Another refinement produced six singleton classes. The failure illus-
trated the mathematical necessity of iterating to a fixed point rather than declaring a one-step
condition sufficient.
A research notebook is improved, rather than discredited, when such mistakes are documented
and explained. Their presence also gives future readers an independent target for verification.

C.3 What a stronger reproducibility audit would require
A complete independent audit should run a unified test suite from a clean environment, iden-
tify the exact source revision for every script, record deterministic outputs and input seeds, and
compare each implementation to an independently written specification. For the proof kernel,
it should include property tests, type-checking negative controls, malformed input handling,
and a clear statement of the trusted computing base. For event logs, it should test duplicated
identifiers, missing dependencies, cyclic causal merges, stale interpretation references, and
inconsistent certificate claims.
The current experiments are sufficient to support the specific finite demonstrations in this
report. They are not sufficient to certify an integrated engine’s security, scalability, or com-
pleteness.

Chapter D
Glossary for the independent reader
Admissible transformation. An operation permitted by the rules and domain of the chosen
mathematical model. Being admissible does not establish that it occurred.
Ancestry or historical continuity. A documented directed path connecting recorded states
or events. It need not be symmetric.
Betti number. A topological invariant measuring independent holes of a given dimension in a
topological space. The first Betti number counts independent loops in the examples used here.
Canonical representative. A chosen standard member of an equivalence class, useful for
comparison. It is not automatically the actual historical member.
Category. A mathematical structure with objects, arrows, identity arrows, and associative
composition. It is used to formalize how transformations can be composed.
Certificate. An object or record that can be checked as evidence for a specified mathematical
property. Its validity is relative to explicit rules and assumptions.
Causal partial order. A relation expressing recorded precedence constraints without assign-
ing an order to every pair of events.
Coherence. Conditions governing whether different ways of composing mathematical com-
parisons yield compatible results.
Congruence. An equivalence relation respected by the operations of a mathematical system.
Confluence. A property of a rewriting system ensuring divergent reductions can be joined by
further reductions.
Counterexample. A specific mathematical construction violating a proposed universal state-
ment, thereby disproving that statement.
Decoder. A function recovering a specified target—such as a state, feature, or equivalence
class—from retained evidence.
Dependency set. The definitions, rules, assumptions, and interpretations used to justify a
particular mathematical result or certificate.
Derived view. A representation computed from a richer original record, such as a final state,
canonical trace, or quotient partition.
Domain of a partial map. The set of inputs on which the operation is defined. Equality of
partial maps requires compatible domains as well as equal outputs.
Endpoint equivalence. Agreement of selected structures at the start and end of a history,
without a claim about intermediate preservation.

Event occurrence. A particular instance of an operation, distinguished from its general type
and from other occurrences of the same type.
Evaluation functor. A map that assigns composite mathematical transformations to ordered
event paths while preserving the operation of composition.
Faithful functor. A functor that does not identify distinct arrows within each source–target
hom-set. The history evaluation functor need not be faithful.
Functor. A structure-preserving map between categories: it sends objects and arrows to ob-
jects and arrows while respecting identities and composition.
Historical authenticity. An evidential claim that an event truly occurred with stated origins,
timing, or authorship. It is not established by formal proof validity alone.
Holonomy. The transformation induced by parallel transport around a loop, which may de-
pend on the chosen path despite identical start and end locations.
Injective map. A map that never sends two distinct inputs to the same output. It is the basic
mathematical condition for exact recovery from a single observation.
Invariant. A quantity or structure preserved under a specified class of transformations.
Isomorphism. A morphism with an inverse. It is the category-theoretic model of structural
equivalence appropriate to the chosen category.
Kernel. For a linear map, the set of inputs mapped to zero. It identifies directions of informa-
tion the map cannot distinguish.
Linear extension. A total ordering of a finite partial order that respects all its precedence
relations.
Normal form. A state or expression to which no further rewrite rule applies. A unique normal
form does not imply a unique reduction history.
Observational equivalence. Agreement under an explicitly chosen observation map. It may
be weaker than full-state equality.
Partial transformation. An operation defined only on a specified subset of possible inputs.
Path. A composable ordered sequence of event occurrences or morphisms. A path is not iden-
tified with its composite effect by default.
Persistence. Continuation of a selected structure along actual transformations. The stronger
Atlas criterion studies invertibility of induced maps at every step.
Proof relevance. Treatment of the identity and structure of proofs as mathematical data,
rather than recording only whether a proposition is provable.
Provenance. The recorded origins, dependencies, transformations, and evidence associated
with an object or claim.
Restoration. Reappearance of a structure at a later state despite a failure of stepwise preser-
vation along the selected history.
Sound checker. A checking system whose accepted derivations establish valid judgments un-
der its stated interpretation and trusted assumptions.
